Plain-language notes for every figure in this folder: what it shows, how to read it, and what it found.
A glossary of the terms used in the figures is at the end.

Figures are PDFs. The numbers behind them, and the run logs, are in `../../data/`. Each script redraws its figure
from there with `--plot-only` (see its docstring). The long-run file behind `tdrjmcmc_accuracy`, `_trajectories`

An honesty audit of all scripts and figures was done on 2026-11-13/14; what it found and what was changed is
listed under **Audit notes** at the end. Old versions of the cost benchmark and t0 scan, replaced after the
audit, are in `../../superseded/`.

## The problem in one paragraph

We look for a short gravitational-wave burst (a "chirp") hidden in noise. Like LIGO's BayesWave, we model the
signal as a sum of **wavelets** (short, smoothly windowed oscillations). The number of wavelets **N** is not
known in advance, so the sampler has to add and remove wavelets as well as adjust them. Its job is to produce
the set of signals consistent with the data (the **posterior**). We judge it by how well its reconstructed
signal matches the signal we injected, and by how quickly it gets there.

The quantum ingredient (**QCLT**) is a way of proposing the next adjustment to a wavelet. It draws a random
step whose size and direction follow that wavelet's own history, generated by a quantum circuit with no
intermediate measurement. **Our method is QCLT + AMR**: the QCLT step acting on small registers (6 bits per
parameter instead of 16) whose grid windows adapt to where the posterior is (adaptive gridlines, AMR).

**Classical or quantum?** Every legend and table says which each result is. "Classical" runs entirely on a
classical computer. "Quantum (simulated)" means the step is drawn from a quantum circuit that is simulated
exactly on a classical computer; no result here comes from quantum hardware. In the samplers only the
adaptive step is quantum; birth/death, tempering and acceptance are classical.

## What each algorithm enables

**Common to all of our samplers** (`tdrjmcmc_qclt.py`):
- trans-dimensional birth/death moves on 20% of moves (TDRJ);
- 8-temperature parallel tempering with non-reversible even/odd swaps;
- an **adaptive temperature ladder**, tuned during burn-in and then frozen, so this half of "APT" is on for
  Fisher and naive too;
- time–frequency redraws on 10% of moves, BayesWave-style SNR amplitude prior, and every parameter stored on
  a 16-bit grid.

| sampler | classical / quantum | adaptive proposal (AP) | QCLT kick | adaptive gridlines (AMR) | history-informed birth | Fisher moves |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive | classical | – | – | – | – | – |
| Fisher | classical | – | – | – | – | every within-model move |
| AP (Tian et al.) | classical | ✓ | – | – | ✓ | warm-up + 20% fallback\* |
| AP, TF birth | classical | ✓ | – | – | – | warm-up + 20% fallback\* |
| QCLT-AP | quantum kick (simulated) | ✓ | ✓, 16-bit registers | – | ✓ | warm-up + 20% fallback\* |
| QCLT-AP, TF birth | quantum kick (simulated) | ✓ | ✓, 16-bit registers | – | – | warm-up + 20% fallback\* |
| **QCLT + AMR (ours)** | quantum kick (simulated) | ✓ | ✓, 6-bit registers | ✓ | ✓ | warm-up + 20% fallback\* |
| **QCLT + AMR, TF birth (ours)** | quantum kick (simulated) | ✓ | ✓, 6-bit registers | ✓ | – | warm-up + 20% fallback\* |
| BayesWave (real) | classical | – | – | – | – | its own proposal set (Fisher-matrix, time–frequency/cluster) |

\* "Warm-up + 20% fallback" means: before the adaptive move switches on (t0), and on 20% of within-model
moves afterwards. In the `apt_*.pdf` figures the warm-up and fallback move is instead the fixed untuned
step (the naive move), so those runs are derivative-free.

- **AP (Tian et al.)** is their full **AP-PT-RJMCMC**: adaptive proposal + adaptive non-reversible
  tempering + trans-dimensional RJ.
- **QCLT-AP** is the same algorithm with the adaptive move's random step generated by the QCLT circuit
  (simulated exactly classically; validated against the circuit). Its registers span each parameter's full
  range at 16 bits.
- **QCLT + AMR (ours)** is QCLT-AP with adaptive gridlines (`propose_ap_qclt_amr` in `tdrjmcmc_qclt.py`; the
  same move, without the gravitational-wave parts, is `qclt_amr.py`). Each time–frequency cell has 6-bit
  registers (64 grid points each) that hold the wavelet's coordinates along the **principal axes** of that
  cell's recent history. The window is centred on the recent mean (exponentially weighted, memory about 200
  states), with about 4 grid points per spread along each axis, so it spans about ±8 spreads. It re-grids
  every 50 history states. It zooms out ×2 while more than 20% of its moves fall outside, and back in below
  5% (the "zoom in & out" strategy of `adaptive_grid.py`). The QCLT kick acts on the 6-bit registers; the
  stored history states are expressed in the same registers. The displacement is round(V (s ⊙ ε)). Its
  distribution is symmetric and does not depend on the current point, and the move is rejected unless both
  ends lie in the window, so it is an ordinary Metropolis move. The map from registers to parameters (centre,
  axes, spacings) is classical. Windows freeze with the rest of the adaptation after burn-in.
  - **Why principal axes:** an earlier version used one register per parameter (axis-aligned windows). It
    fails on strongly correlated posteriors: a grid spaced by each parameter's own spread cannot resolve a
    narrow ridge running diagonally across the axes, so every step leaves the ridge (acceptance 0 on the
    correlated Gaussian of `applications_accuracy.pdf`). The qubit count is the same either way.
  - **Checks:** the likelihood-off test recovers the prior (N, the SNR prior, and the uniform priors of t0,
    f0, Q and φ), with independent chains used for the error bars. The kick circuit needs 260 qubits instead
    of 610 in the original five-parameter sampler. Current circuit counts, including zero-mean and Cauchy–Schwarz
    methods, are in [`method_costs.tex`](../../paper/figures/method_costs.tex).
- **Fisher and naive** correspond to Tian et al.'s PT-RJMCMC, plus the adaptive temperature ladder.
- **BayesWave** (default settings) also does trans-dimensional moves with 20 tempered chains and an adaptive
  ladder; it additionally fits the noise spectrum and samples sky/polarisation. **BayesWave, matched
  settings** (`bayeswave_matched.py`) uses 8 chains, keeps the noise spectrum fixed and fixes the sky at the
  detector's zenith, to separate BayesWave's settings from its sampler. Its wavelet Fisher matrix is
  closed-form (its `SineGaussianFisher` routine calls only exp/sin/cos/atan/sqrt), so its Fisher moves cost no
  extra waveform evaluations; ours use finite differences.

## Figures

### Covariance estimator diagnostics
Methods: the max / min (Cauchy–Schwarz ends) and zero-mean shortcuts; the **standard SWAP-test estimator**
(Lloyd, Mohseni & Rebentrost 2013, the distance estimation used in quantum k-means); and the CLT walk,
classical and quantum. A plain SWAP test between two amplitude-encoded columns gives only |⟨a|b⟩|², i.e.
the correlation without its sign. The standard fix, used here, is a SWAP test between (|0⟩|â⟩ + |1⟩|b̂⟩)/√2
and (‖a‖|0⟩ − ‖b‖|1⟩)/√Z, which gives ‖a − b‖² and so the signed covariance. Like all amplitude encoding,
it needs the centred columns and their norms classically, so its variances come for free. These are now three
standalone plots; qubit counts are in the TeX table above.
- [Shot error](cov_methods_shot_error.pdf): error against the number of measurement shots. The "max", "min" and "zero-mean" shortcuts level
  off at a large built-in error. The SWAP test and the CLT walks keep improving as 1/√shots. The SWAP test
  needs about 20× more shots than the CLT walks for the same error (median 0.053 vs 0.012 at 2¹⁴ shots).
- [Dataset error](cov_methods_dataset_error.pdf): the same comparison over 300 random datasets.
- [CLT step kurtosis](cov_methods_clt_kurtosis.pdf): Gaussianity of the quantum CLT steps as a function of coin probability $q$.
- **What the CLT curves mean.** The CLT walks never form an estimate Ĉ: each kick already has covariance
  exactly ∝ S (checked to 1e-14 against the circuit). Their curves show how closely the empirical covariance
  of B kicks matches S, i.e. the ordinary 1/√B sampling noise. The walk only needs the right covariance per
  step, so this understates rather than overstates the CLT approach. The "classical CLT" computes S on a
  classical computer first; it is the reference for what the circuit loses, not a quantum method.
- **How it was checked.** Methods 1–3 and the SWAP test: circuit probabilities match Aer statevector
  simulation to 1e-10 (SWAP test: 8e-16);
  shot noise is then drawn as Binomial(shots, p). Quantum CLT: sampled from the circuit by an exact
  simulator for its gate class, validated against Aer in `quantum-cov-rw.ipynb` (total-variation distance
  3e-16 for the kick, 2e-16 for a small full walk).

### `tdrjmcmc_qclt.pdf`: main sampler comparison (SNR-20 chirp)
16 runs each of seven samplers: Fisher (classical); AP (classical), QCLT-AP and **QCLT + AMR (ours)** with
Tian et al.'s history-informed birth; and the same three with the ordinary time–frequency ("TF") birth.
QCLT-AP and QCLT + AMR use a simulated quantum kick. The adaptive move switches on at iteration 1000 and
adaptation is frozen at 20k. Real BayesWave (4 runs each with default and with matched settings, same chirp
and SNR) is overlaid in (a) and (b). All 16 runs of ours analyse one noise realisation: this figure studies
one posterior, so run-to-run spread here is sampler spread only. The
x-axis counts model updates per chain: one of our iterations is 1 update per chain, one BayesWave
iteration is 100.
- **(a)** Match against model updates. All of ours reach about 0.94 within about 10³ updates; BayesWave
  (both settings) reaches about 0.92 after about 10⁶. The 0.94 vs 0.92 gap is not a sampler difference: our
  runs share one noise realisation that happens to be favourable. With one realisation per run
  (`snr_sweep.pdf`), ours also end at 0.92 at SNR 20.
- **QCLT + AMR matches the others on this posterior** with 6-bit instead of 16-bit registers: final match
  0.937 (others 0.932–0.938), N re-explored as fast, and 1/120 run pairs agreeing by Tian et al.'s test
  (AP and QCLT-AP 0/120, Fisher 10/120).
- **(b)** Number of wavelets N. AP, QCLT-AP and QCLT + AMR with the history-informed birth hold N near 4.3 while
  adapting, then jump to about 5.3 once adaptation is frozen. That is a bias of the adaptive birth: it was
  also caught by the likelihood-off prior test, and is why adaptation is frozen after burn-in. The TF-birth
  variants track Fisher throughout. BayesWave uses fewer wavelets (about 3–4); its priors favour simpler
  models.
- **(c)** How long two independent runs of ours take to agree, by Tian et al.'s test (both agreement scores
  below 0.2). The number under each box is how many of the 120 run pairs ever agreed; within 40k
  iterations only Fisher pairs did (10/120), plus one pair of each QCLT + AMR variant.
  **Why so few:** the test has two scores. R_c1 compares the two runs' posterior mean waveforms and mostly
  passes. R_c2 compares their posterior spreads, averaged over every time sample of the 0.4–0.6 s window,
  and almost never passes. It fails in the quiet half of the window, where the spread is tiny and is set by
  occasional weak wavelets fitting noise: in the 100k runs R_c2 is 0.11–0.17 over the louder half of the
  window but 0.31–0.48 over the quieter half. Whether a run carries such a wavelet changes only when N
  changes, and N is re-explored only about 0.5–0.8 times per 1000 iterations. So 40k iterations hold too
  few independent values of N for two runs to agree on the spread, though they agree on the signal and
  reach the same match. At 100k iterations 32% of Fisher pairs pass (AP 0%, QCLT-AP 4%). Passing would
  need several hundred thousand iterations, or faster mixing in N. This test compares per-sample waveforms, which BayesWave does
  not save, so BayesWave cannot be shown here.
- **(d)** How fast the number of wavelets is re-explored: independent samples of N per 1000 model updates per
  chain (higher is better, log scale). All of ours are similar, about 1–2. BayesWave (from its saved
  samples) manages 0.001–0.02, i.e. 100–1000× fewer per update. Per CPU second the two are comparable
  (ours 0.24–0.34, BayesWave 0.05–1.3), because BayesWave's updates are much cheaper than ours.
- These panels count **iterations**, which favours Fisher: each Fisher iteration of ours costs about 20 extra
  waveform evaluations for finite-difference derivatives. Per waveform evaluation see `apt_cost.pdf`:
  finite-difference Fisher is the most expensive sampler, but Fisher with closed-form derivatives is the
  cheapest.

### `tdrjmcmc_accuracy.pdf`: how accurate does the answer get? (8 runs × 100k iterations of ours)
- **(a)** The averaged reconstruction compared with the injected signal. It levels off at 0.25 for every
  sampler. That floor is set by the noise in the data, not by the sampler.
- **(b)** The averaged reconstruction compared with the true posterior (the answer an ideal sampler would
  give). This is the sampler's own error, and it keeps falling as 1/√iterations.
- **(c)** How far the current sample is from the injected signal (1 − match), with real BayesWave for
  comparison (its posterior median at saved checkpoints).
- **(d)** Tian et al.'s two-run agreement scores; the horizontal line is their 0.2 threshold. R_c1
  (means, solid) gets below it; R_c2 (spreads, dotted) does not within 100k iterations, for the reason given
  under `tdrjmcmc_qclt.pdf` (c).

### `tdrjmcmc_trajectories.pdf`, `tdrjmcmc_burnin.pdf`: what the walk does
One run per sampler on the SNR-20 chirp, plus one real BayesWave run.
- **Trajectories, top row:** where each wavelet sits in the time–frequency plane over the run (colour = model
  updates so far), drawn over the data's signal power, with the injected chirp dashed.
- **Trajectories, bottom row:** the reconstructed waveform (median and 90% band) against the injected
  signal. BayesWave analyses LIGO-coloured noise, so its reconstruction is shown whitened and rescaled to

- **Burn-in:** every parameter of every wavelet, against model updates on a log axis, so the early

### `snr_sweep.pdf`: real BayesWave vs our samplers at SNR 10, 20, 40, 80
Real BayesWave 1.1.3 (LIGO's code, installed from conda-forge) analyses the chirp in simulated Advanced
LIGO noise. The chirp is coloured by the noise spectrum, so that after whitening it is the same waveform our
samplers see in white noise. BayesWave runs 200k iterations with its default 20 temperatures, and, at SNR 20
and 40, also with matched settings (8 chains, noise spectrum and sky fixed). Ours run 40k iterations with 8
temperatures. Four runs per SNR per sampler, **each on its own noise realisation**. AP and QCLT-AP here use
the Fisher move as their warm-up and fallback move, as in Tian et al.
- **Top row:** match against model updates per chain (one BayesWave iteration = 100 updates).
- **Middle row:** match against CPU seconds on one core. BayesWave is compiled C; ours is Python.
- **Bottom:** final match against SNR.

| final match (median of 4) | SNR 10 | SNR 20 | SNR 40 | SNR 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BayesWave, default settings | 0.68 | 0.92 | 0.98 | 0.99 |
| BayesWave, matched settings | – | 0.92 | 0.98 | – |
| Fisher (ours) | 0.73 | 0.92 | 0.98 | 0.99 |
| AP (ours) | 0.73 | 0.92 | 0.98 | 0.995 |
| QCLT-AP (ours) | 0.74 | 0.93 | 0.98 | 0.99 |

- **Accuracy: at SNR ≥ 20 every sampler, ours and BayesWave, ends at the same match** (within 0.01, less
  than the run-to-run spread). At SNR 10 ours end higher (0.73 vs 0.68), with a wide spread for everyone
  (single runs 0.55–0.82). BayesWave's priors favour fewer wavelets when the signal is weak.
- **Earlier numbers were optimistic.** Before the audit all four runs of ours shared one noise realisation,
  and that one happened to be favourable: 0.77 at SNR 10 and 0.94 at SNR 20. With one realisation per run they
  are 0.73 and 0.92.
- **The posterior is not dragged down by a tail of bad samples**, for us or BayesWave. At SNR ≥ 20 no
  posterior sample of either has match below 0.8. At SNR 10, 75% of ours and 91% of BayesWave's are below 0.8,
  and 1–2% of ours and 6% of BayesWave's are below 0.5. Low match there is the noise, not stray wavelets.
- **Speed:** ours reach their plateau after about 10³–10⁴ updates per chain. BayesWave needs about
  2–5 × 10⁵. In CPU seconds the two are within a factor of a few.
- **Caveats:** BayesWave's default also fits the noise spectrum and the sky/polarisation parameters, and is
  tuned for long production runs. Its priors and search ranges are wider than ours (see the audit notes).
  So this is a comparison of samplers on the same signal, not a claim that ours replaces BayesWave.

### `apt_cost.pdf`, `apt_t0.pdf`, `apt_tempering.pdf`: is adaptive parallel tempering worth it?
One study (`apt_study.py`, 512 runs) answers three questions: what each sampler costs, when the adaptive
proposal should switch on, and what the adaptive temperature ladder adds. Two cases, both at SNR 40, 8 runs
per sampler, **each run on its own noise realisation**:
- **easy**: the short chirp, new wavelets placed where the data has power (TF birth);
- **hard**: a wide chirp plus an extra burst, new wavelets placed at random (prior birth).

The cost unit is the **waveform evaluation**: on a quantum computer each is an oracle call. All of them are
counted, including the 8192 spent once on the time–frequency birth map in the easy case. AP and QCLT-AP are
derivative-free here: before t0, and on 20% of moves afterwards, they use the naive step. "Reaches 0.95"
means the median match over the second half of the run so far reaches 0.95: the same definition as
BayesWave's checkpoint values.

**`apt_cost.pdf`**: match against waveform evaluations (a, d) and CPU time (b, e); evaluations needed to
reach 0.95, one dot per run (c, f). t0 = 300 for AP and QCLT-AP.

| evaluations to reach match 0.95 (median of 8) | easy case | hard case |
|---|---|---|
| Naive (fixed random steps) | 32,000 | 460,000 (7/8 runs got there) |
| Fisher, finite-difference derivatives (as run) | 75,000 | 950,000 |
| Fisher, if derivatives were free | **12,300** | **56,000** |
| AP (Tian et al.) | 20,700 | 175,000 |
| QCLT-AP (quantum) | 30,000 | 232,000 |
| BayesWave, default settings (≈) | 4,000,000 | 20,000,000 (3/4) |
| BayesWave, matched settings (≈) | 2,800,000 | 12,000,000 |

- **Against naive steps, the adaptive proposal pays off on the hard case**: AP needs 1.5× (easy) and 2.6×
  (hard) fewer evaluations. QCLT-AP needs 2.0× fewer on the hard case but is no better than naive steps on
  the easy case (1.1×, within the scatter).
- **QCLT-AP costs more than classical AP**: 1.4× (easy) and 1.3× (hard) at t0 = 300; over all ten
  (case, t0) settings of `apt_t0.pdf` about 1.25× on average (range 0.9–1.7×, more expensive in 9 of 10).
  Its lazy three-point coin leaves most pairs unmoved, so the steps are less Gaussian.
- **Against Fisher, it depends entirely on how derivatives are obtained.** With finite differences (what our
  code does) Fisher is the most expensive sampler. With closed-form derivatives, which exist for these
  wavelets and which BayesWave uses, Fisher is the cheapest: 1.7× (easy) and 3.1× (hard) fewer evaluations
  than AP. In the easy case two thirds of its cost is the one-off birth map. **The earlier claim "QCLT-AP
  needs about 4× fewer evaluations than Fisher" depended on finite differences and is withdrawn.** It
  would hold only for a model whose derivatives are expensive.
- **BayesWave uses one to two orders of magnitude more** (40–300× depending on which of ours). Its counts are approximate (model updates × chains, see the
  audit notes), its prior ranges are wider than ours, and it saves a sample only every 100 iterations, so
  its cost is known only to within a factor of about 2.5 (the checkpoint spacing). With matched settings it
  is somewhat cheaper than with its defaults and reaches the same accuracy.
- **CPU time** (single core) is a different story: our Python AP bookkeeping is slow, so naive and Fisher are
  fastest, and BayesWave (compiled C) is in the same range as ours.

**`apt_t0.pdf`**: cost to reach 0.95 against t0, the iteration at which the adaptive proposal switches on
(median and interquartile range of 8 runs; horizontal lines are the samplers without an adaptive proposal).
- **There is no penalty for switching on early.** In the hard case every t0 from 10 to 3000 gives the same
  cost within the scatter. In the easy case the cost creeps up with t0, and t0 = 3000 is clearly too late:
  by then most of the work is done with naive steps.
- AP beats naive steps at every t0. QCLT-AP does on the hard case, but on the easy case it is only level with
  naive steps (within 20%). Fisher with free derivatives beats both at every t0. In iterations (left
  panels) Fisher is 3–7× ahead of everything.
- This replaces the earlier t0 scan, whose "switching on early never helps" came from measuring iterations
  (not evaluations) with a Fisher warm-up move, all on one noise realisation.

**`apt_tempering.pdf`**: the other half of "APT", the adaptive temperature ladder (tuned during burn-in, then
frozen), against a fixed geometric ladder with the same 8 temperatures and the same hottest temperature.
- **The effect is small: a null result.** The medians differ by at most about 30%, less than the run-to-run
  scatter. In the hard case the adaptive ladder is slightly cheaper for Fisher, AP and QCLT-AP; in the easy
  case there is no consistent difference. 8 runs per setting cannot resolve effects this small.
- **BayesWave shows the same.** Matched-settings BayesWave with its ladder frozen at its initial spacing
  (`--noAdaptTemperature`, 4 runs per case) reaches 0.95 as fast or faster than with adaptation, and ends at
  the same match (differences are within its checkpoint spacing).
- **This is not visible elsewhere:** every other sampler figure uses the adaptive ladder throughout, so this
  is the only place the ladder itself is tested.

### `adaptive_grid_*.pdf`: adaptive gridlines (AMR) on their own (all classical)
**Every walk in these figures is a classical simulation; none was run as a quantum circuit.** They test the
gridline strategies that QCLT + AMR uses, on problems small enough to score exactly. Each parameter is stored
in an n-bit register: 2ⁿ allowed values ("grid points") spread over a window. On a quantum computer that would
be n qubits, so register size is the quantum resource being saved. With the window fixed at the full allowed
range, precision is limited by the spacing. An adaptive grid moves and resizes the window as the walk learns
where the probability is, so a few bits can still reach high precision. The likelihood is evaluated only at
grid points, as a quantum oracle would see it.

Strategies compared (all classical):
| name | what it does |
|---|---|
| fixed grid | window = full range, n bits (baseline) |
| fixed grid, 16 bits | the "just add bits" reference, with ±1-grid-point steps |
| 16 bits + adaptive step | the fair "just add bits" reference: 16 bits over the full range, and steps of 2ᵐ grid points with m re-chosen every block by the same spread rule the zoom strategies use |
| zoom in only | shrink the window to K × the walk's recent spread, never widen |
| zoom in & out | resize to K × recent spread in either direction, and widen ×2 if the walk presses against the edges |
| … + look-around | every 4 re-grids, search the full range for one block; move only if it found a better point |
| several windows + look-around | keep up to 4 windows at once (a small "which window" register); a look-around that finds a good point outside them opens a new window there |

Every walk proposes either a step of ±1 grid point per parameter (the QCLT three-point coin) or, 5% of the
time, a jump to a uniformly random grid point. On a quantum computer that jump is a Hadamard on the register:
cheap for a small register, and the only way across an empty gap between peaks. The wavelet phase is periodic
and is treated as such (windows may wrap around it). The wavelet walks start at the peak of the data's
time–frequency map, as a data-driven birth would.

- **AMR examples:** separate plots: [narrow peak](adaptive_grid_example_narrow_zoom_inout.pdf), [zoom-in trap](adaptive_grid_example_decoy_zoom_in.pdf), [look-around](adaptive_grid_example_decoy_restart.pdf), and [multiple windows](adaptive_grid_example_decoy_multiwin.pdf).
- **AMR comparisons:** separate plots for [narrow error](adaptive_grid_narrow_error.pdf), [decoy recovery](adaptive_grid_decoy_recovery.pdf), [wavelet error](adaptive_grid_wavelet_error.pdf), and [register precision](adaptive_grid_register_precision.pdf).
- **Window scale $K$:** separate [decoy](adaptive_grid_K_decoy.pdf) and [narrow-peak](adaptive_grid_K_narrow.pdf) plots. The window width is $K$ times recent spread, swept from 3
  to 50, with 4 bits per parameter.
  - **Up to K ≈ 20, wider is better on both counts:** every strategy finds the decoy's true peak, and
    precision improves to about 0.03 spreads.
  - **Past that it collapses:** at K = 32–50 the error jumps to about 2 spreads, as bad as a fixed grid,
    and decoys are missed again. The 16 grid points are then spaced wider than the peak, which slips
    between them.
  - **Rule of thumb: make each grid point cover about one spread of the walk,** i.e. K ≈ 2ⁿ (16–20 for 4
    bits). (QCLT + AMR in the sampler uses finer windows, about 4 grid points per spread, because there the
    grid sets the step size of the QCLT kick, not the resolution of the state.)

| final result (median of 12 runs, K = 8; all classical) | narrow peak error | wavelet error | decoy: runs that found the true peak | decoy: time in true peak (correct 0.70) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fixed grid | 2.1 | 32 | 58% | 0.57 |
| fixed grid, 16 bits | 1.9 | 40 | 92% | 0.87 |
| 16 bits + adaptive step | 0.083 | 4.9 | 75% | 0.63 |
| zoom in only | 0.072 | 5.7 | 17% | 0.00 |
| zoom in & out | 0.071 | **0.61** | 17% | 0.00 |
| zoom in & out + look-around | 0.077 | 1.4 | **100%** | 1.00 |
| several windows + look-around | **0.048** | 1.1 | **100%** | **0.84** |

- **Against a fair baseline:** on the simple 2-D peak, 16 bits with an adaptive step does as well as the
  zoom strategies; there the only gain from AMR is register size (4 bits instead of 16). On the 5-parameter
  wavelet, AMR with 5 bits is 3–8× more accurate than 16 bits with an adaptive step.
- **Trapping:** zooming in without any way back out gets trapped by a decoy. A look-around that only moves
  when it finds a better point fixes that, but one window cannot represent two separate peaks.
- **Several windows** is the only strategy that finds the true peak and keeps the decoy as well. It is the
  closest to the correct 70/30 split, though still biased towards the dominant peak because jumps between
  windows are rare.
- **Practical recommendation:** use zoom in & out with the window sized so that one grid point ≈ one spread
  (K ≈ 2ⁿ). Add a look-around that only moves to better points, and keep several windows when the answer may
  have more than one peak.

## Glossary

| term | meaning |
|---|---|
| **SNR** | signal-to-noise ratio: how loud the signal is compared with the noise. LIGO detections are typically about 10–40. |
| **wavelet** | one short windowed oscillation, described by 5 numbers: central time t0, central frequency f0, quality factor Q (how many cycles it lasts), amplitude A, and phase φ. |
| **N** | number of wavelets in the model at a given moment. |
| **match** | normalised overlap between the reconstructed and the injected signal. 1 = identical, 0 = unrelated. BayesWave calls it *overlap*. |
| **posterior** | the set of signals consistent with the data, weighted by how plausible each one is; it is what the sampler is trying to draw from. |
| **burn-in** | the start of a run, before the sampler has found the signal; these samples are discarded. |
| **iteration** | one round in which every chain proposes one change. One BayesWave iteration is 100 such changes. |
| **TD-RJMCMC** | trans-dimensional reversible-jump Markov chain Monte Carlo: a random walk over models that can also change N. Our Python sampler is one; BayesWave is another. |
| **birth / death** | the moves that add or remove a wavelet. |
| **TF, time–frequency** | the plane with time on one axis and frequency on the other. "TF birth" places new wavelets where the data has the most power in that plane; "prior birth" places them at random. |
| **parallel tempering (PT)** | several copies of the sampler run at different "temperatures". Hot copies explore freely, cold copies sample the posterior, and they swap states now and then. |
| **APT** | adaptive parallel tempering: the step shapes (and here the temperatures) are tuned on the fly from the run's own history. |
| **Fisher move** | a step shaped by the local curvature of the likelihood. Accurate, but each move needs the waveform's derivatives, about 20 extra waveform evaluations. |
| **AP (Tian et al.)** | adaptive proposal: the step shape comes from the history of past states, a running covariance. |
| **QCLT** | quantum central-limit-theorem step: the same idea as AP, but the random step is built by a quantum circuit from differences of stored past states. |
| **t0** | iteration at which the adaptive move switches on (before that, conventional moves are used). |
| **covariance** | how much each parameter varies, and how pairs of parameters vary together. |
| **ESS** | effective sample size: how many independent samples a correlated chain is worth. |
| **R-hat / two-run agreement** | checks of whether independent runs have reached the same answer. Values near 1 (R-hat), or below 0.2 (Tian et al.'s scores), mean they agree. |
| **waveform evaluation** | computing one wavelet's waveform. On a quantum computer this would be an oracle call, so it is the natural cost unit. |
| **qubits per parameter / grid** | each parameter is stored in a small register: n qubits give 2ⁿ allowed values (grid points). |

## Audit notes

A line-by-line check of whether each figure shows what it claims, done after the results looked "too good to
be true". What was found, and what was done:

| # | finding | effect | fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | All runs of ours used one noise realisation; each BayesWave run had its own. | spread between our runs understated; comparisons with BayesWave not like-for-like | cross-sampler studies (`apt_*`, `snr_sweep`) now give every run its own realisation (`data_seed = 1000 + seed`). Single-posterior studies (`tdrjmcmc_qclt`, `_accuracy`, `_trajectories`, `_burnin`) keep one realisation on purpose, and now say so. |
| 2 | The 8192 waveform evaluations of the time–frequency birth map were not counted. | TF-birth samplers looked cheaper than they are | counted from evaluation 0 (`evals_setup`); prior-birth runs compute no map. |
| 3 | "Reaches 0.95" was the first iteration whose instantaneous match touched 0.95. | optimistic, and not the same quantity as BayesWave's posterior median | median over the second half of the run so far, as BayesWave's checkpoints. |
| 4 | Our Fisher moves use finite-difference derivatives (2 × 5 = 10 evaluations per Fisher matrix, about 20 per move). For these wavelets the Fisher matrix exists in closed form; BayesWave's does (its `SineGaussianFisher` calls only exp/sin/cos/atan/sqrt). | "QCLT-AP is 4× cheaper than Fisher" was an artefact | Fisher shown both as run and "if derivatives were free"; claim withdrawn. |
| 5 | Adaptive grid, wavelet test: walks started at the truth + a small offset. | head start no real run gets | start at the peak of the data's time–frequency map. Also added a fair "16 bits + adaptive step" baseline: plain 16-bit grids took ±1-grid-point steps of 1/65536 of the range, which cannot move. |
| 6 | Adaptive grid, wavelet test: the phase φ was treated as an ordinary interval, not as periodic; the reference answer also cut the posterior at the seam. Found when fix 5 moved the start. | walks reaching the posterior from across the seam were pinned against the edge (errors of ~12 spreads); the old results were right only because the old start approached from the good side | phase periodic everywhere: wrapping windows, ring-shaped full-range registers, circular statistics, wrapped reference chain and error metric. (The TD-RJMCMC samplers always treated φ as periodic.) |

Remaining caveats, disclosed rather than fixed:
- **BayesWave's evaluation counts are approximate**: model updates × chains, one evaluation per update.
  Updates rejected by the prior need none; extrinsic and noise-model updates need more. BayesWave saves a
  sample every 100 iterations, so its curves start at 200 iterations (those first points rest on 1–2 samples)
  and its "reaches 0.95" is known only to the checkpoint spacing (about 2.5×).
- **Priors and noise differ.** BayesWave: 1 s window, 32–512 Hz, Q up to 40, up to 100 wavelets with its own
  wavelet-count prior, LIGO-coloured noise. Ours: 0.8 s, 30–400 Hz, Q 2–30, up to 10 (easy) or 20 (hard)
  wavelets with a uniform prior on N, white noise with the same whitened signal. Matches are computed the same
  way (whitened overlap).
- **The QCLT kick inside the sampler is simulated, not run on a quantum device.** Its distribution is
  drawn exactly by a classical sampler for the circuit's gate class, which is validated against Aer in
  `quantum-cov-rw.ipynb`. The small isotropic lattice term and the bookkeeping of the stored history are
  classical in `tdrjmcmc_qclt.py`. The kick probability is q = s_c · s_d / 30 (6 stored states); when the
  adaptive scale s_c exceeds about 26, q is capped at 1 (`tdrjmcmc_qclt.py:339`), so those kicks are smaller
  than the classical AP's step at the same s_c.
- **QCLT + AMR's windows adapt only during burn-in.** Like every adaptive part of our samplers, they are
  frozen after the adaptation period, so the sampler is exact afterwards. During burn-in it is an adaptive
  MCMC, as Tian et al.'s AP is.
- **CPU times** are single-core: Python for ours, compiled C for BayesWave. They measure implementations,
  not algorithms. In `apt_cost.pdf` our CPU time includes building the birth map; at intermediate points it
  is the run's total scaled by the fraction of iterations done.
